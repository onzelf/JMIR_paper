\section{Introduction}
Digital health and clinical artificial intelligence increasingly depend on access to diverse, multimodal, and representative data distributed across organisations and care settings. Models developed or validated within a single institution may not perform consistently across different populations or operational contexts, whereas broader cross-organisational collaboration can improve robustness and generalisability while reducing dependence on narrowly defined local datasets [Li et al., 2025; Bradley et al., 2016; Trinh et al., 2025].

Despite substantial investment in digital health and artificial intelligence, many initiatives that show promising results in pilot settings do not progress to sustainable operational services [Topol, 2019; Gandhi et al., 2024]. Explanations commonly emphasise model performance, data quality, clinical validation, and local implementation barriers, but operationalisation may also require the collaboration itself to change. Adding an organisation can entail further contracting, governance review, technical integration, and assurance, and large-scale health-data programmes have identified organisational and technical integration as important sources of delay [Goldacre \& Morley, 2022].

Pilots can usually operate within a predefined collaborative arrangement in which participating organisations, datasets, analytical objectives, and responsibilities are established before execution and remain relatively stable. Operational services must accommodate changes in participation, available data, and analytical capabilities as needs and circumstances evolve. Scaling therefore concerns not only the execution of distributed computation but also the ability of a governed collaboration to evolve without reconstructing its organisational and technical arrangements for each new activity.

Existing health-data infrastructures address important parts of this problem at different boundaries. Centralised platforms provide common custody and access mechanisms, Trusted Research Environments provide controlled analytical access to governed datasets, federated learning frameworks coordinate computation across organisational boundaries while keeping raw data local, and data-space or policy frameworks establish requirements for participation and data sovereignty. Their functions remain necessary, yet the decision that a participant may enter a particular federated activity under a defined authority, purpose, and set of capabilities can remain distributed across contracts, accreditation, identity systems, application configuration, and operational controls.

OpenHealth addresses the transition from fixed collaboration to evolving service through Federated Computing, which represents collaboration as a governed structure within which autonomous organisations retain sovereignty over local data and systems while participating under shared constitutional rules. Federated Computing is intended to complement rather than replace health-data platforms, identity systems, federated learning frameworks, or organisation-local security controls by providing the governance structure through which participation can become legitimate within an existing federation.

Session-bound admission provides one mechanism for establishing such participation. Admission is evaluated for a specific governed activity, purpose, and bounded set of capabilities rather than treated as a persistent property of an authenticated organisation or user. Different participation relations can consequently coexist within the same federation. An organisation may, for example, contribute to federated training without acquiring the rights of an existing federation member, and permission to contribute need not imply permission to consume the resulting model. Collaboration growth can therefore proceed through purpose-specific and asymmetric participation relations while preserving the governance conditions of the federation.

The OpenHealth demonstrator examines this proposition in a multisite federated learning setting using PathMNIST. An initial federation comprising Hospitals A and B is extended through the governed participation of Hospital C as a sponsored contributor. Hospital C may contribute to federated training but may not consume the resulting model solely by virtue of that contribution. The implementation provides a controlled setting in which to examine whether federation participation can expand while preserving explicit authority, capability boundaries, session binding, and auditable decision evidence.

The study addresses the following research question.

\textit{Can an open federated health infrastructure support the governed evolution of a collaboration by admitting new forms of participation while preserving bounded capabilities and auditable decision evidence?}

The paper makes three contributions. It first reframes the transition from pilot to service as a problem of governed federation evolution rather than solely one of computational or data scale. It then presents an OpenHealth implementation of Federated Computing in which session-bound admission, capability separation, and auditable decision evidence are integrated with a conventional federated learning runtime. Finally, it evaluates governed federation expansion through a controlled scenario in which an additional organisation contributes to model training without acquiring unrestricted federation rights.

The remainder of the paper reviews existing collaborative health-data approaches and the governance boundaries they address, introduces the Federated Computing principles used by OpenHealth, and describes the implementation and experimental design. Subsequent sections report the evaluation results and discuss their implications, limitations, and directions for further development.

\section{Existing Solutions and Related Work}

\subsection{Collaborative health-data infrastructures}

Health-data collaboration is supported by several classes of infrastructure, including centralised data platforms, Trusted Research Environments (TREs), proprietary federated platforms, open-source federated learning frameworks, and policy or data-sovereignty frameworks. They are not direct substitutes because each establishes a different boundary around data custody, computation, participation, and governance.

Centralised data platforms simplify integration by bringing data under a common technical and administrative environment, where repository-level identity, access-control, and data-management mechanisms can govern users and datasets. Such an arrangement is effective when common custody is organisationally and legally acceptable but is less suitable when participating organisations must retain direct sovereignty over sensitive health data. TREs adopt a different boundary by providing controlled environments in which approved researchers or projects analyse specified datasets under explicit governance and disclosure controls. OpenSAFELY is a prominent example. TREs therefore provide strong mechanisms for research approval, controlled access, and accountability while principally governing access to approved data and projects rather than the continuing evolution of a federation of autonomous organisations and operational relationships.

Proprietary integration and federated platforms, including Rhino Health, Syntropy, and the Mayo Clinic Platform, support cross-site analytics while allowing participating organisations to retain local data. Their governance, deployment, and operational mechanisms are defined within the corresponding platform environment. Open-source federated learning frameworks such as Flower, NVIDIA FLARE, Intel OpenFL, PySyft, and Substra similarly provide computational mechanisms for coordinating distributed machine-learning workflows while keeping raw data local. In both cases, federated computation can be technically coordinated while authority to participate remains represented through platform governance, deployment configuration, or surrounding organisational processes.

Cross-organisational collaboration also depends on semantic interoperability because participating institutions may represent clinically equivalent concepts through different data structures, terminologies, coding systems, or information models. Shared vocabularies and ontologies can make distributed data interpretable across organisational boundaries, but semantic correspondence does not determine whether a participant is entitled to contribute a resource, consume a resulting model, or perform an operation under a particular authority. Semantic interoperability and federation governance are therefore complementary requirements rather than alternative solutions to the same problem.

Policy-oriented data-space initiatives, including Gaia-X, the European Health Data Space (EHDS), and TEHDAS, address trustworthy exchange, organisational eligibility, interoperability, and data sovereignty at ecosystem level. Their emphasis on organisational control is closely aligned with cross-organisational health-data collaboration, although eligibility for an ecosystem does not by itself establish entitlement to every activity performed within it. Conversely, an operational federation-level admission decision cannot replace the regulatory, contractual, ethical, or organisational conditions that established eligibility.

AI-agent frameworks introduce a further implementation layer by coordinating how computational participants invoke tools, services, workflows, memory, or other runtime resources. Tool restrictions, isolation, approval mechanisms, and monitoring can constrain execution, but such controls do not determine whether an AI-driven process is entitled to participate in a federated health-data activity, under whose authority it acts, or which federation capabilities it may exercise. OpenHealth therefore treats the agent framework as an execution mechanism whose implementation may change without redefining the governance semantics of the federation.

\subsection{Governance boundaries in cross-organisational collaboration}

Cross-organisational infrastructures depend on established identity and access-control mechanisms. Federated identity systems can authenticate principals across organisational boundaries, while OAuth-based mechanisms, cloud IAM, RBAC, and ABAC can control access to services and resources. Such technologies provide necessary deployment foundations by establishing whether an identity can be trusted, whether a service account may invoke an API, or whether an actor possessing specified attributes may access a resource. Participation in a shared activity raises a different question because the relevant decision must be established across organisational boundaries.

In operational deployments, the effective participation decision may be distributed across cloud IAM policies, platform configuration, central policy services, contractual arrangements, and checks embedded in orchestration code. Heterogeneous identity and infrastructure stacks can make integration increasingly bespoke as collaborations evolve, while configuration changes can alter effective participation semantics and complicate reconstruction of earlier decisions. Organisation-local access control and cross-organisational admission consequently address different governance boundaries. The former controls activity within an administrative domain, whereas the latter establishes whether a participant may enter a specified collaborative activity under the applicable federation conditions.

Cross-silo federated learning illustrates the separation between computational coordination and participation governance. An FL framework can coordinate model distribution, local training, update submission, aggregation, and evaluation while leaving the authority for participation external to the learning procedure. Prior work on auditable session admission has shown that requests can be evaluated against session-specific capabilities and holder identity before reaching the FL runtime, producing explicit ALLOW or DENY decisions and structured decision evidence without modifying the underlying training and aggregation procedure [Fenoglio \& Treleaven, 2026]. OpenHealth adopts the separation between governance boundary and analytical runtime while applying it to the broader problem of federation evolution.

A health organisation may therefore be authenticated, satisfy regulatory or data-space eligibility requirements, operate infrastructure interoperable with an FL platform, and grant its users valid local permissions without those conditions alone determining whether the organisation, user, or computational process may perform a particular federation activity for a specified purpose and bounded capability. OpenHealth addresses that transition by making the participation decision explicit, session-bound, and auditable while leaving data custody, local identity, operational authorisation, semantic interoperability, and analytical execution with the mechanisms suited to those functions.

\subsection{Dimensions of collaboration growth}

Scaling a collaboration requires changes not only in the number or type of participants but also in the resources available to the federation and the operations participants may perform. The three dimensions are related but governed through different relations. New participants establish participation relations, new datasets or models extend the federation's resource base, and new analytical capabilities extend the set of permitted operations. Distinguishing them avoids collapsing heterogeneous entities and activities into a single category of objects to be admitted.

\section{OpenHealth as a Federated Computing Infrastructure}

\subsection{Federated Computing and the governed federation context}

OpenHealth applies Federated Computing to the problem of evolving cross-organisational health-data collaboration. Federated Computing treats the federation as a governed structure rather than as a collection of connected technical endpoints. Participating organisations retain sovereignty over their local data, infrastructure, identities, and internal access mechanisms, while the federation establishes the shared conditions under which cross-organisational participation becomes legitimate. The resulting boundary is complementary to organisation-local IAM, RBAC or ABAC, Trusted Research Environments, federated learning frameworks, and other operational controls because those mechanisms continue to govern activity within their respective administrative or computational domains.

A governed federation context is established before operational execution. The context identifies the authorities participating in the collaboration, the applicable constitutional and policy state, the purpose of the activity, and the conditions under which participation may occur. OpenHealth represents the bounded context through a federation envelope, allowing later capability and admission decisions to be interpreted relative to the governance state under which they were made. Authentication alone therefore does not establish federation participation, nor does prior organisational eligibility imply entitlement to every subsequent activity.

The architecture separates governance establishment, capability expression, and operational execution. Governance establishment defines the conditions under which the collaborative context exists. Capability expression describes the bounded activities that a participant may request within that context. Operational execution performs the corresponding computation after admission. Keeping the three concerns distinct allows the participation structure to change without requiring the analytical runtime or organisation-local security mechanisms to become the representation of federation governance. It also prevents an operational configuration change from silently redefining the constitutive conditions of participation.

Federated Computing consequently provides a structure in which collaboration can evolve through changes in relations among participants, resources, and operations. A new organisation need not acquire the rights of an existing member, a newly contributed resource need not become available to every participant, and permission to perform one operation need not imply permission to perform another. The relevant governance question is therefore not whether an entity has been globally admitted to the federation, but whether a particular participation relation is legitimate within the governed context.

\subsection{Session-bound admission}

Session-bound admission operationalises the transition from an established governance context to a requested federated activity. The admission decision asks whether a specific participant may perform a specified operation, for a defined purpose and scope, within the current governed context. An ALLOW or DENY outcome is produced before the requested activity reaches the corresponding analytical or operational runtime. Prior work on auditable session admission demonstrated that such a boundary can be inserted before a federated learning session without modifying the underlying training and aggregation procedure [Fenoglio \& Treleaven, 2026]. OpenHealth retains the boundary-oriented principle while applying it to a broader federation in which participation relations may change over time.

Capabilities used at the admission boundary are contextual rather than global. A capability issued for one governed context does not constitute a reusable entitlement to arbitrary future activities, and possession of a capability is not equivalent to federation membership. Admission evaluates the holder, requested operation, purpose, resource and scope against the capability and the governance state associated with the federation envelope. The analytical runtime remains responsible for executing an admitted activity but does not determine the legitimacy of the participation relation that preceded it.

\subsubsection{Admission of participants}

A participant may be a human actor or a conventional software process acting within the federation under an accountable organisational relationship. The issuer expresses the participant's permitted federation activity through a bounded capability associated with the current federation envelope. Holder binding connects the capability to the participant for whom it was issued, while purpose, operation, resource, and scope delimit the activity that may subsequently be requested. The gatekeeper then evaluates the presented capability against the current governed context rather than treating issuance itself as an admission decision.

Separating issuance from admission supports differentiated participation. An established member may hold capabilities for both training and model use, whereas another organisation may receive only a contribution capability for a specified federated training activity. Sponsorship provides an accountable route through which such participation can be established without transferring the sponsor's rights to the sponsored participant. Sponsorship is therefore distinct from delegation, and the existence of a sponsorship relation does not convert the sponsored participant into an equivalent federation member.

The same separation applies across capability categories. Permission to contribute a resource or model update does not imply permission to consume the resulting model, and admission to one operation does not establish unrestricted participation in other operations. Provenance similarly records where a participant, credential, model, or other artefact originates, whereas accountability concerns the authority under which participation occurs and the party responsible for that relationship. Treating provenance and accountability as separate relations avoids inferring federation rights from technical origin or organisational association alone.

Admission decisions also produce explicit governance evidence. The evidence associates the participant, governed context, requested activity, capability, purpose, decision outcome, and reason for the decision, allowing successful and denied requests to be reconstructed from the federation boundary rather than inferred solely from application logs or the historical configuration of several independent systems. Auditable decision evidence therefore links governance intent to operational participation without requiring centralisation of organisation-local policy mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Admission of AI agents}

AI agents do not require a separate constitutive governance category. From the perspective of Federated Computing, an AI agent is a form of computational participant acting under an accountable organisational relationship. The model provider, planning strategy, memory architecture, tool framework, or execution runtime may affect how the agent behaves, but none of those implementation properties independently determines its federation rights. Admission remains governed through the same relations of authority, purpose, capability, holder binding, and federation context that apply to other participants.

Agent participation nevertheless introduces an additional operational distinction between runtime capability and federation capability. An agent runtime may expose file access, network communication, tools, APIs, or other execution mechanisms, but technical reachability does not establish entitlement to use a federation resource. Conversely, admission to a bounded federation operation does not grant unrestricted authority over the agent's local runtime or external environment. The federation therefore governs whether the computational participant may cross a particular boundary, while the agent runtime governs how admitted execution is carried out.

Admission cannot certify that an AI agent will behave correctly after execution begins. Sandboxing, network isolation, tool policies, approval mechanisms, monitoring, and other runtime controls remain complementary mechanisms for constraining operational behaviour. Their presence does not expand the participation relation established by the federation, and an execution framework must not silently redefine federation semantics merely because a technically reachable action is available. The relevant assurance requirement is therefore conformance between the operational possibilities exposed to the agent and the capabilities established by the governed admission decision. Section 4 evaluates this requirement through a bounded AI-agent participation scenario while preserving the same federation-governance model used for human and conventional computational participants.

\section{OpenHealth PoC Implementation }

\subsection{System architecture }

The OpenHealth proof of concept (PoC) implements a governed federated learning environment in which participation is established for a specific federation session before computation begins. The implementation separates the governance and admission plane from the federated learning runtime. This allows the conditions under which an organisation or actor participates to be evaluated independently from the technology executing the subsequent computation. 

The architecture contains four functional areas: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the user and federation-authority interfaces  
\item the OpenHealth governance and orchestration services  
\item the federated learning runtime  
\item the participating hospital sites  
\end{enumerate}

The separation between these areas is intentional. OpenHealth does not replace the federated learning framework. Flower remains responsible for distributed model training and aggregation. OpenHealth determines whether participation in a particular federation session is legitimate and under which capabilities that participation may occur.

\subsection{Federation context and session-bound admission }

Admission in OpenHealth is not a persistent statement that an actor is generally trusted or authorised. It is evaluated relative to a specific governed federation context. 

Before a federated activity begins, the federation authority creates an envelope representing the session-specific governance context. The envelope identifies the context in which subsequent participation decisions are valid and binds the activity to the applicable constitutional and policy state. 

The envelope\_id therefore acts as the admission binding for the session. This distinction is central to the implementation. Authentication establishes who an actor is. A capability credential represents what that actor may request. Neither independently establishes the right to participate in an arbitrary federated activity. Admission is evaluated when the capability is presented within the governed context identified by the envelope. 

The resulting relation can be expressed conceptually as: identity + capability + governed session context → ALLOW or DENY. The decision is consequently contextual rather than global. A capability valid for one federation context does not imply unrestricted participation outside that context. 

This is what makes the mechanism session-bound admission rather than conventional account-level access control. 

\subsection{Governance establishment }

The federation authority establishes the governance context before operational execution. In the proof of concept, this process includes creation of the envelope and explicit binding and approval of the governance context. The approval process is protected by the governance mechanisms implemented in OpenHealth, including the KYO-based binding procedure and the required approvals. 

The resulting governance state provides the basis against which later admission requests are evaluated. The governance state contains the relevant constitutional and policy information together with the active envelopes and the evidence generated by admission decisions. These artefacts are maintained independently from the Flower runtime. This separation preserves an important architectural invariant. The runtime does not define the legitimacy of participation. It executes computation only after that legitimacy has been established by the governance layer. 

\subsection{ Capability issuance }

Participants interact with the federation through explicitly issued capability credentials. The OpenHealth issuer service produces an ECT, the capability credential used by the proof of concept. The ECT is bound to the relevant governance context and expresses the capabilities available to the holder, including the permitted resource, operation, purpose, and applicable scope. 

The ECT is not itself an admission decision. It provides evidence that a participant possesses a capability that may be presented for admission. The gatekeeper subsequently evaluates that capability in the context of the requested activity. This distinction prevents capability issuance from becoming equivalent to unrestricted federation membership. A participant may therefore hold a narrowly scoped capability without acquiring other capabilities available to existing federation members. We exploit this property directly in the Hospital C experiment. 

\subsection{ Admission gateway }

The verifier and gatekeeper constitute the admission boundary of the OpenHealth proof of concept. When a participant requests entry into a governed activity, the gatekeeper evaluates the request against: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the participant identity  

 \item the presented ECT  

 \item the envelope\_id  

 \item the requested operation  

 \item the stated purpose  

 \item the requested resource and scope  

 \item the applicable policy and constitutional state  
\end{itemize}

The result is an explicit ALLOW or DENY decision produced before the requested computation is executed. 

The gatekeeper also generates evidence describing the decision and its reason. Consequently, both successful and unsuccessful admission attempts become observable governance events rather than implicit outcomes of runtime configuration. 

This is different from authenticating a Flower client or checking a role after execution has already entered the federated runtime. The OpenHealth decision establishes whether that participant may enter the specified activity at all. 

\subsection{User and orchestration layer }

Users interact with the proof of concept through the React/Vite dashboard that separates federation administration from participant operations. Requests are routed through the Nginx gateway to the corresponding OpenHealth services. 

The fc-hub FastAPI service provides the orchestration layer. It coordinates the governed workflow and maintains the association between the current federation context and the operational federated-learning run. 

The orchestration layer does not itself determine policy. Admission decisions remain the responsibility of the verifier and gatekeeper. 

Similarly, the dashboard is not the source of governance truth. It exposes the state and operations of the underlying services. 

This distinction is important because the PoC is intended to demonstrate governance enforced by the system rather than governance represented only in a user interface. 

\subsection{Governance state and evidence }

Governance state is maintained separately from the federated model and training runtime. 

As illustrated in Figure 1, the verifier has access to the constitutional and policy artefacts required for admission evaluation. The implementation also maintains the active federation envelopes and the resulting decision evidence. 

For every evaluated request, the evidence record captures the relevant decision context, including the actor, requested capability, purpose, scope, decision outcome, and reason. 

The evidence trail therefore allows a later observer to distinguish: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item who requested participation  

\item under which governed session  

\item for which operation  

\item for which purpose  

\item with which capability  

\item whether participation was admitted  

\item why the decision was reached  
\end{itemize}

This evidence is produced by the admission process rather than inferred retrospectively from application logs. 

That is an important part of the PoC claim. 

 

\subsection{Run registry and model association }

The proof of concept maintains an explicit association between the governed federation context and the corresponding model run. 

The run registry and model pointer shown in Figure 1 identify the operational model associated with the current federated activity. This allows the governance layer and the federated runtime to remain separate while retaining a traceable relationship between them. 

The distinction between session admission and model execution is deliberate. 

The capability credential is bound to the governed federation context rather than being treated as an unrestricted token for arbitrary future model runs. Where a request concerns a particular executed run, the corresponding admission decision can additionally record that run association. 

This avoids collapsing three different concepts into one object: 

governed session, capability, and executed model run. 

 

\subsection{Federated learning runtime }

Once the required participation has been admitted, the operational computation is performed using Flower. 

The Flower server coordinates federated training rounds and aggregates updates received from participating clients. Each hospital operates its own Flower client and trainer. 

PathMNIST data remain within the corresponding hospital environment. The federated workflow exchanges the information required for model training rather than transferring the underlying raw images between participating hospitals. 

The Flower runtime is therefore intentionally conventional. 

The contribution of OpenHealth is not a new federated learning algorithm. It is the governance structure surrounding participation in that computation. 

This architectural separation also makes the approach potentially applicable to analytical runtimes other than Flower. 

 

\subsection{ Baseline federation }

The initial experimental configuration consists of Hospitals A and B. 

Both participate as federation members and operate local PathMNIST partitions. Their Flower clients contribute to the federated training workflow according to the capabilities established by the federation. 

This configuration provides the A+B baseline. The corresponding model is trained without data from Hospital C and provides the reference against which the later federation expansion is evaluated. 

The data partitioning intentionally results in weaker representation of selected PathMNIST classes (colon cancer data vs normal tissue) in the A+B configuration. This gives the later Hospital C contribution an observable analytical role rather than making the additional participant merely decorative. 

 

\subsection{Sponsored contribution by Hospital C }

The second configuration introduces Hospital C without converting it into an equivalent federation member. 

Hospital C participates through a sponsorship relation established by an existing federation participant. The sponsored contributor receives a capability permitting participation in federated training through the submit\_update operation for the specified training purpose. 

The capability does not include model-consumption rights. Consequently, the following two propositions are simultaneously true: 

Hospital C may contribute to the federated model. 

Hospital C may not query or consume that model merely because its contribution was admitted. 

This asymmetric participation relation is intentional. 

It demonstrates that federation growth need not be implemented by adding progressively more actors with identical privileges. OpenHealth permits a collaboration to expand through differentiated relations whose capabilities correspond to their purpose. 

The Hospital C experiment therefore tests more than technical onboarding. It tests whether the federation can change its participation structure while preserving explicit capability boundaries. 

 

\subsection{Controlled A+B versus A+B+C experiment }

The proof of concept compares three federation configurations. 

\subsubsection{A+B baseline }

Hospitals A and B train the federated model using their local PathMNIST partitions. 

\subsubsection{A+B+C governed expansion with a sponsred guest (Mode 1A) }

Hospital C participates as a sponsored contributor after successful session-bound admission and contributes additional training data while retaining its restricted capability set. 

Hospital C was allocated substantial representation of selected PathMNIST classes that were intentionally weakly represented in the A+B baseline. The expanded federation therefore provides a controlled means of examining whether a governed change in participation produces an observable change in the resulting model. 

The experiment has two independent outcomes: (i) The first is a governance outcome. Hospital C must be capable of contributing while remaining unable to perform operations outside its granted capability; (ii) the second is an analytical outcome. The A+B+C model can be compared with the A+B baseline to determine whether the additional data contribution alters model performance. These outcomes must not be conflated. Improved model performance does not validate the governance mechanism, and successful governance does not imply improved clinical performance.

\subsection{What the proof of concept establishes }

The PoC is designed to establish three architectural properties: (i) First, federation participation can be made session-specific rather than treated as a persistent property of an authenticated actor; (ii) second, a federation can expand through asymmetric participation relations. A contributor need not acquire the rights of a full member; (iii) third, admission decisions can be made before computation and retained as explicit evidence linked to the governed context in which they were made. 

The experiment does not evaluate clinical effectiveness, production-scale deployment, or general health-system outcomes. Those questions remain outside the scope of this PoC.
